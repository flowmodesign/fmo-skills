# deck narrative: story structure for a deck

The rest of this package covers how a deck looks (type floor, one accent,
copy bans, the designer-page rendition's fixed stage). This file covers what
a deck says and in what order: choose a strategy from the goal, lay out the
slides along an emotion arc, place the pattern breaks, and write each slide
with the copy formula that fits its job.

## Step 1: name the goal, pick the strategy

Write one line before any slide: "This deck exists to get \<audience> to
\<decision or belief> by \<the end of the meeting / reading it alone>."
Then pick the closest strategy below. Slide counts are typical, not fixed;
cut slides before adding them.

| Goal | Strategy | Typical length |
|---|---|---|
| Raise a first round | Seed pitch | 10-12 |
| Raise a growth round | Series A | 12-15 |
| Close a qualified buyer | Sales pitch | 8-10 |
| Report a period to leadership or a board | QBR / update | 8-12 |
| Prove value with one customer's story | Case study | 7-10 |
| Announce something new | Product launch | 8-12 |
| Win approval for a plan inside the company | Internal proposal | 6-10 |

### Seed pitch

Order: hook (one sentence on what this is) -> the problem, told through one
real person or moment -> why now -> the product -> early proof (users,
pilots, letters of intent, whatever is true) -> market size, bottom-up ->
how it makes money -> team and why them -> the ask and what it buys.

Arc: curiosity -> recognition of the pain -> relief -> belief -> trust in
the people -> momentum toward yes.

### Series A

Order: where the company is today in one number -> the problem as now
proven by customers -> the product and what changed since seed -> traction
over time (cohorts, retention, revenue) -> what drives growth -> market and
expansion path -> competition and the edge -> unit economics -> team and
key hires -> the plan for the round -> the ask.

Arc: confidence -> validation -> scale -> defensibility -> conviction.

### Sales pitch

Order: their situation (show you did the homework) -> the problem in their
words -> the cost of leaving it alone -> the solution, shown working ->
proof from someone like them -> why this over the alternatives -> price
framed against the cost of inaction -> the likely objections, answered ->
the next step with a date.

Arc: being understood -> discomfort -> worry -> hope -> trust -> ease ->
commitment.

### QBR / update

Order: the headline result for the period -> goals versus actuals ->
what worked and why -> what did not and what was learned -> key metrics
trend -> risks and blockers -> priorities for the next period -> the
decisions or help needed from this room.

Arc: orientation -> honesty -> credibility -> clarity -> a clear request.
An update deck is not a sales deck: bad news goes early and plainly.

### Case study

Order: the customer and their stakes -> the situation before -> what they
tried that failed -> the turning point (choosing this) -> what was done ->
the measured result -> their own words -> what this means for the viewer.

Arc: identification -> tension -> turning point -> payoff -> "this could
be us".

### Product launch

Order: the shift in the world that makes this necessary -> the old way and
its pain -> the reveal -> three things it does, each shown -> who it is
for -> availability, pricing, how to start -> the one line to remember.

Arc: anticipation -> frustration -> surprise -> delight -> clarity ->
desire to act.

### Internal proposal

Order: the recommendation up front -> the problem and the cost of not
acting -> the options considered -> why this option -> what it takes
(people, money, time) -> risks and how they are handled -> success
measures -> the decision needed today.

Arc: clarity -> concern -> fairness -> confidence -> a decision. Lead with
the answer; decision makers should not wait until slide 9 to learn what is
being asked.

## Step 2: alternate "what is" and "what could be"

Within the arc, move back and forth between the current reality (the pain,
the old way, the numbers today) and the possible future (the product
working, the target state). Each swing raises the contrast between the two;
the deck ends on the new normal with a concrete step to reach it. A deck
that states the problem once and then only lists features loses that
tension after slide 3.

## Step 3: place the pattern breaks

Attention sags in long runs of same-shaped slides. Plan one deliberate
change of pace around the one-third mark and another around the two-thirds
mark: a full-bleed image, a single huge number, a live demo or video, a
customer quote alone on the slide, a question to the room, a section
divider in a different ground color. The break should carry content (the
strongest proof point is a good candidate), not be decoration. Keep the
rest of the slides in a steady rhythm so the breaks read as breaks.

## Step 4: write each slide

Rules for every slide:

- **One idea per slide.** If a slide needs two headlines, it is two slides.
- **The title states the takeaway**, as a full short sentence: "Churn fell
  by a third after onboarding changed", not "Churn" or "Q3 metrics".
  Someone reading only the titles should get the whole argument.
- **The body proves the title**: one chart, one image, one quote, or up to
  three short points. Nothing on the slide that the title does not need.
- Same copy bans as pages: no em-dashes, no filler verbs, no unsourced
  precise stats.

Copy formulas, matched to the slide's job:

| Formula | Shape | Use on |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem, agitate, solve** | Name the pain, show what it costs or how it spreads, then present the fix | Problem slides, the opening of a sales pitch |
| **Attention, interest, desire, action** | A sharp opening claim, a detail that makes it relevant, a reason to want it, a single next step | Hook slides, closing / CTA slides |
| **Feature, advantage, benefit** | What it is, what that lets you do, what you get from it | Product and feature slides |
| **Before, after, bridge** | Life before, life after, the product as the way across | Solution slides, case-study turning points, launch reveals |
| **Cost of inaction** | The status quo, what it loses each week or month, how that compounds | Agitation slides, the price slide of a sales pitch, internal proposals |

Pick one formula per slide; mixing two on one slide is a sign it holds two
ideas.

## Step 5: check the deck as a whole

- Read only the titles in order: do they tell the full story and land on
  the ask?
- Mark each slide "what is" or "what could be": do they alternate, and does
  the deck end on "could be" with a concrete step?
- Are there pattern breaks near the one-third and two-thirds marks?
- Does each slide follow the page rules for layout variety (no run of
  identical three-card slides) and keep the deck to one accent strategy?
- Would the deck still make sense to someone reading it alone, without the
  presenter? If it is meant to be sent, titles and short body copy must
  carry what the speaker would have said.
